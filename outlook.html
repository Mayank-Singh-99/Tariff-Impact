Forward view

What comes after “settled at 10%”

The duty schedule stopped moving in July 2026 and the immediate question changed. Not how bad is the tariff, but what does an Indian exporter do with a 10% US duty, a set of new trade agreements, a currency at record lows, and an unresolved allegation that Indian firms are moving Chinese goods? These are the four answers worth taking seriously, and none of them is a forecast.

How to read these

The percentages below are author-assigned weights, not model outputs and not probabilities derived from data. They are a way of saying which futures deserve attention. Two of the four are not mutually exclusive: an external shock can arrive on top of a durable reset, which is why the weights do not sum to 100. Treat them as emphasis, not as a distribution. The reasoning and the triggers are the useful part.

Relative emphasis

Where the attention goes

The two most-weighted futures are the current state of affairs continuing, and an external shock landing on top of it. Both are continuations of things already happening, which is the uncomfortable reading of the evidence.

The short version

  • The most likely future is the one already in place: a 10% duty, flat US exports, and growth coming from somewhere else.
  • The largest downside is not American. It is oil, shipping, and rare earth inputs, none of which Washington controls.
  • The most underrated risk is enforcement: a transshipment determination aimed partly at India would hit legitimate exporters hardest.
  • A genuine détente is possible but would need an agricultural purchase India does not want to make.

In detail

The diversification that actually happened

Four agreements signed in the space of a year

India concluded or entered into force more trade agreements in 2025-26 than in the preceding two decades combined. That is the single largest change in Indian trade policy in this period, and it is worth being precise about what it does and does not do. None of these deals restores US market access. They redistribute the risk of not having it.

The arithmetic nobody wants to do

The UK agreement is the one to watch, because it is a large high-income market with an apparel sector that genuinely competes with India’s. The EFTA deal’s projected effects are the most ambitious on this page, but they are projections produced by negotiators, not observed outcomes. The honest summary is that India has bought a great deal of optionality, mostly in Europe, the Gulf and the Pacific, and that optionality is worth something only if the firms can afford to use it. Compliance costs, not tariffs, are the binding constraint on smaller exporters, which is what the six Textile Export Facilitation Centres are actually trying to fix.

What to watch

The signals that would tell you which future is arriving

Rather than a forecast, these are the observable events that separate one scenario from another. Each has already appeared once in this episode, so each has a known tell.

A duty schedule that moves again

Any new executive order, Section 301 modification, or a court decision that reinstates a country-specific rate. The pattern so far is that the schedule changes when the politics change, not when the trade data do. Watch the rate series.

Brent and Cape of Good Hope

India imports about 90% of its crude, and a rerouted Cape of Good Hope voyage adds roughly two weeks of sailing. Oil and freight together are the transmission mechanism that actually moves the rupee and the Reserve Bank.

A transshipment determination

A published finding naming Indian firms or the Pune–Gujarat–Chennai corridor. A determination is what turns an allegation into a duty, and the current claim is still an allegation.

US apparel import data by origin

Whether India’s roughly $11bn share holds against Bangladesh, Vietnam, Cambodia and Indonesia while they enjoy the quota carve-out. This is the cleanest available read on whether the differential treatment is biting.

The rare earth truce expiry

The November 2025 China–US arrangement on rare earths and soybean purchases was due to expire in November 2026, with both sides negotiating an extension as of September 2026. A collapse would hit Indian input supply, not Indian exports.

RoDTEP and the duty arithmetic

The RoDTEP rebate was cut, restored, and then extended. Its restoration is what makes the 10% Section 301 levy roughly neutral for Indian exporters, which means the neutrality depends on a domestic programme that can be withdrawn.
