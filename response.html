India’s response

Four tracks, and a deliberate decision to wait

India’s response is best understood as restraint rather than retaliation. New Delhi litigated, negotiated, subsidised its exporters and diversified its markets — and then declined to sign a deal it considered unfavourable, even after absorbing five and a half months at a 50% tariff.

The position, in one quotation

Commerce and Industry Minister Piyush Goyal, at UK-India Week 2026: “It’s all about comparative advantage… The day that happens, the deal is on.” India would come into force its trade pact only after securing a clear tariff advantage over Vietnam, Thailand, the Philippines, China, Malaysia, Bangladesh and Sri Lanka.

Track one

Legal and institutional

India took the measures to the WTO and engaged formally in the US domestic process, without treating either as an alternative to negotiation.

WTO dispute filings

Notification stage

India informed the WTO of its intention to impose retaliatory tariffs on select American goods in response to US duties on Indian steel and aluminium. It maintains those US measures are not consistent with GATT 1994 and the Agreement on Safeguards. The US position is that the duties rest on national-security grounds and should not be regarded as safeguards — a framing that would place them outside standard WTO disciplines.

Section 301 engagement

Ongoing

The USTR concluded investigations against 60 economies, including India, on whether they had taken sufficient action against goods allegedly produced using forced labour. India participated in the process, with written comments accepted until 6 July 2026 and public hearings on 7 July 2026. A proposed textile and apparel mechanism would let a volume of imports from selected economies in at lower rates; India was outside it.

Framework agreement track

Ready, unsigned

Announced 2 February 2026 and confirmed in a joint statement on 7 February 2026, the framework removed the Russian-oil penalty and cut the reciprocal rate to 18%. Trade Secretary Rajesh Agrawal has since said the framework deal is ready and “whenever it is the right time, it will be signed”. The US Ambassador to India, Sergio Gor, said most issues were resolved.

Rejection of the capacity charge

Contested

Washington prepared further tariffs on the basis of investigations into excess industrial capacity. India has rejected these allegations outright. This is a substantive disagreement about industrial policy, not a bargaining position, and it was not resolved by the February 2026 framework.

Track two

Negotiating: what India insisted on

After months of talks, the two sides failed to finalise an interim agreement during USTR Jamieson Greer’s visit to New Delhi in June 2026, despite expectations that a limited deal was within reach.

India’s demands

  • Comparative advantage. A rate below every major Asian competitor. Goyal: “The day that happens, the deal is on.”
  • No new levies after signing. A guarantee that the agreement is not followed by fresh US duties.
  • Agriculture and dairy. Identified as red lines, to be defended even at the cost of the deal.
  • Durability. Confidence undermined when the Supreme Court struck down the underlying framework in February 2026.

India’s leverage

  • The February 2026 Supreme Court ruling removed the legal basis for country-specific rates, weakening the coercion.
  • Indian officials believe some remaining US measures could also face legal or political setbacks.
  • Falling oil prices and a growing services surplus reduce external vulnerability.
  • Alternative partners and pending FTAs reduce dependence on the US market.
  • Exports ticked up and India’s economic risks eased while Washington waited.

“Our position is clear — we don’t intend to rush into a deal that is not on favourable terms or compromise on red lines like ceding ground on agriculture.”

Indian government official familiar with the talks, quoted by Reuters, July 2026

What India offered, and what it got in return

India committed to increase purchases of US petroleum, defence equipment, electronics, pharmaceuticals, telecom products and aircraft, alongside tariff concessions on alcoholic beverages, cosmetics, medical devices and specific agri-inputs, with safeguards such as minimum import prices. The framework was reported to unlock roughly $500 billion of stated purchasing intent.

On the other side, the deal opened a path for US firms into India’s nuclear power sector, enabled by the SHANTI Act 2025, as well as defence manufacturing, and deepened the iCET initiative on critical and emerging technology.

Track three

Domestic support for exporters

The most revealing domestic episode was the RoDTEP round trip: cut, reversed, extended. It shows the government discovering and then correcting the cost of raising exporters’ own burden.

23 Feb 2026

RoDTEP rates halved

Withdrawn

DGFT Notification 60/2025-26 limited all RoDTEP rates and value caps in Appendices 4R and 4RE to 50% of notified levels, with immediate effect. Products in HS Chapters 01 to 24 were exempt. The Economic Times reported the move landed while global demand was already weak.

23 Mar 2026

Cut reversed in full

Restored

Notification 66/2025-26 withdrew the reduction entirely, restoring the rates and value caps in force on 22 February 2026. Exporters’ guidance: claim RoDTEP at full rates, not at half.

31 Mar 2026

Scheme extended to September

Extended

Notification 74/2025-26 continued RoDTEP for a further six months, 1 April to 30 September 2026, at rates unchanged from 31 March 2026. Extensions have run in six-month tranches rather than long horizons.

Budget 2026-27

RoDTEP proposed for consolidation

Pending

The budget proposes converging RoDTEP into the Export Promotion Mission after appraisal and approval, with the FY 2026-27 RoDTEP allocation cut to ₹10,000 crore.

Export Promotion Mission

Announced in the Union Budget 2025-26, the Mission represents a major structural reform: it merges multiple fragmented export-support initiatives into a single, outcome-based, digitally enabled framework, addressing access to affordable trade finance, market access and infrastructure support.

It builds on existing instruments rather than replacing them — the Press Information Bureau describes interest equalisation, market access initiatives and export-incentive schemes as predecessors.

Remission schemes still running

RoSCTL was extended for textiles, alongside RoDTEP and duty drawback. Duty drawback and EPCG remain claimable in parallel with RoDTEP in most cases, and dual claiming usually maximises the rebate for finished goods with meaningful BCD incidence on inputs.

RoDTEP does not cover GST refunds, which run through a separate claim route, nor input customs duty, which is handled by drawback. The 50% cut exempted only HS Chapters 01 to 24.

The tension worth naming

These measures reduce the cost of exporting, but they also reduce the competitiveness that the tariffs were aimed at. RoDTEP and duty drawback subsidise precisely the embedded levies that the US cites as evidence of India’s high tariff structure. India is simultaneously defending a right to subsidise its exporters and being penalised for doing so — which is one reason the domestic support track and the negotiating track have not always pointed the same way.

Track four

Diversifying away from the US market

The most durable response. Textile exports grew in more than 120 destinations between April 2025 and February 2026, and the composition of India’s export growth shifted permanently.

Broader growth beyond the US also included Australia and Canada in gems and jewellery, Hong Kong in jewellery, and China, Vietnam and the EU in seafood. Shrimp exports to China reached 490,369 MT, making it the largest destination by volume.

⚖

Free trade agreements

The anticipated implementation of FTAs with the UK and the EU in 2026 is expected by GJEPC to further support gems and jewellery export growth, and gives India a market-access route that does not run through Washington.

⚖

Strategic opening

The framework paves the way for US firms to enter India’s nuclear power sector under the SHANTI Act 2025, and into defence manufacturing, deepening the iCET initiative on critical and emerging technology.

⚖

Non-tariff barriers

Diversifying away from the US does not mean escaping friction. US sanitary and phytosanitary standards continue to act as invisible walls for Indian food and pharma exports, and the same barriers exist in the new markets India is redirecting toward.

Assessment

What worked, and what did not

What held

  • Survival of the export base. Merchandise exports grew 17.04% for April–July 2026-27 despite the tariff episode.
  • Services as a shock absorber. A $49.43B surplus in Q1 2026-27, entirely outside tariff scope.
  • Macroeconomic stability. CAD projected at 0.9% of GDP, with export exposure only about 2% of GDP.
  • Sector carve-outs won. Generics, smartphones, steel, aluminium and auto parts excluded from the July 2026 levy.
  • Diversification delivered. 120+ growing destinations, and a record seafood year.

What did not

  • The textile quota exclusion. India pays the full 10% while four rivals may pay less, a structural disadvantage in an $11B market.
  • No final agreement. The framework has been unsigned since February 2026, leaving the rate exposed to further change.
  • The surcharge problem. The 100% ceiling under the Lindsey Graham Act remains available to Washington.
  • Domestic cost cutting. The RoDTEP episode showed the government willing to raise exporters’ own costs, and reversed only after 28 days.
  • Gems and jewellery. A five-year low, with US shipments down 45% and no full recovery to date.

The strategic read

India’s position was that a deal signed under duress would cost more than the tariffs it was meant to avoid. Ajay Srivastava of GTRI framed the same conclusion: “India realises that delaying — or even abandoning — a rushed deal may be more prudent than locking into obligations whose costs could far exceed any temporary tariff relief.” Whether that judgement is correct depends almost entirely on whether the Section 301 duties hold, which was itself under appeal at the time of writing.
