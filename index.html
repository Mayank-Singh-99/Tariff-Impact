April 2025 – September 2026

The US tariff shock to India,
and what New Delhi did about it

In eighteen months, the additional US duty on Indian goods went from nothing to 50% and back down to 10%, passing through a court ruling, a framework deal and three separate tariff statutes along the way. This site sets out what happened, what it cost, and how India responded — with every figure traced to a source.

The rate, over time

Eighteen months of escalation, reversal and litigation

The additional duty is a step function, not a trend. It jumped to 26% overnight, doubled to 50% in late August 2025, halved in February 2026 under a framework deal, and was then overridden by a court ruling and rebuilt under a different statute.

What happened

Three things the tariff episode actually did

Most of the commentary treats this as a single event. It is better understood as three overlapping processes, each with its own logic and its own timeline.

⚠

A demand shock, unevenly distributed

Gems and jewellery exports fell to a five-year low with US shipments down 45%. Textiles grew 2.1% and seafood hit a record $8.46B. The same policy produced opposite outcomes depending on sector and on how quickly a firm could redirect volume to another market.

⚖

A relative-competitiveness problem

The damage was as much about rivals as about the US. India was excluded from the 2026 textile tariff-rate quotas available to Bangladesh, Cambodia, Indonesia and Malaysia, and CareEdge notes its tariff advantage over several Asian peers effectively reversed.

⚖

A negotiation India chose to delay

Having absorbed the 50% peak, New Delhi hardened its terms: a tariff advantage over China and neighbours, and a guarantee against new levies after signing. The framework has been “ready” since February 2026 and remains unsigned.

The bill

Estimates vary by a factor of more than two

There is no single authoritative number for what the tariffs cost India. Trade bodies, rating agencies and economists measure different things. All of them are reported below rather than reconciled into a figure that would be false precision.

Why the headline numbers differ

FIEO works from share of shipments exposed and applies it to the export base, which produces a large absolute number. CareEdge works from India’s total GDP and the observation that merchandise export exposure to the US is only about 2% of GDP, which produces a small one. GTRI extends the exposed set to roughly 70% of US-bound goods, including categories that the Commerce Ministry’s carve-outs have kept out of scope. None of these is wrong; they are answering different questions.

India’s response

Four tracks, run in parallel

New Delhi did not choose between litigation and negotiation. It ran both, plus a domestic support track and a diversification track, and the results of each shaped the others.

⚖

Legal

Notified the WTO of retaliatory tariffs against US steel and aluminium duties, arguing inconsistency with GATT 1994 and the Agreement on Safeguards.

Details →
⚖

Negotiating

A framework ready since February 2026 and still unsigned, held back until India secures a tariff advantage over China and a guarantee against new levies.

Terms →
⚖

Domestic support

RoDTEP halved in February 2026 and restored in full a month later; the scheme was extended to 30 September, and the Export Promotion Mission consolidates fragmented incentives.

Measures →
⚖

Diversification

Textile exports grew in more than 120 destinations, with FTAs pending in the UK and the EU. Sudan, Senegal, Egypt and Nigeria led the growth rates.

The one-line version of India’s position

Commerce and Industry Minister Piyush Goyal, at UK-India Week 2026: “It’s all about comparative advantage… The day that happens, the deal is on.” The condition has not been met, and the deal has not happened.
