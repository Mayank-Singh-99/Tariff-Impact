Impact & consequences

What the tariffs actually did to India

The macro damage was contained by design, because India’s exposure is small and its economy is domestic-driven. The commercial damage was not. This page separates the two, then works down from the total to individual sectors, competitors and the external balance.

Macro

A contained macro hit and a concentrated commercial one

These two statements are both true, and the apparent contradiction is the single most important thing to understand about the episode.

CareEdge Ratings put the direct export loss from the higher US tariffs at 0.3–0.4% of GDP, noting that India’s overall export dependence is relatively low and that merchandise export exposure to the US is only around 2% of GDP. Other agencies estimated the hit to FY26 GDP growth at 0.5% to nearly 1%. On any of these measures the economy absorbed the shock.

The commercial arithmetic is less comfortable. FIEO estimated the impact at $47.5 billion, about ₹4.2 lakh crore, hitting as much as 55% of India’s exports to the US. GTRI, a New Delhi think tank, put the figure higher still at 66%, or over ₹5 lakh crore. Against an $87 billion base, losing more than half of it is a severe outcome for the exporters involved, even though it moves national GDP very little.

That gap is the whole story of why India could absorb the tariffs. Exposure is roughly 2% of GDP, so a large percentage loss on that sliver is a small percentage loss overall. Services exports, which the tariffs never covered, continued to support the external sector and produced a $49.43 billion surplus in the April–June 2026 quarter.

Tariff incidence is not exporter incidence

The headline rate is what the importer pays at the border. What an Indian exporter actually loses depends on how much is absorbed by squeezed margins, currency moves, or simply not shipped. The gap between those two numbers is why credible estimates differ by more than a factor of two.

What was actually exposed

India shipped $87 billion of merchandise to the US in FY25. The composition matters more than the total, because it determines which sectors faced the tariff and which were carved out of it.

Composition of India’s merchandise exports to the US, FY25
Electronic goods, pharmaceuticals and gems & jewellery together made up roughly 41% of the basket.

Two observations follow. First, concentration: the US accounted for 37% of India’s total electronic exports and 35% of India’s total pharma exports. Second, protection: both of those sectors, the second and third largest lines in the basket, were repeatedly exempted, as were smartphones, generic drugs, steel, aluminium and auto parts. Gems & jewellery, the fourth-largest, was not.

The one-line explanation of the sectoral pattern

The sectors that India found hardest to protect politically — gems, gems and jewellery, textiles, shrimp — were the ones that got hurt. The sectors with strong lobbies and USFDA or defence supply chains — generics, electronics — were shielded. Tariff outcomes track domestic interest groups on both sides of the Pacific.

Who is exposed under the current regime

Under the Section 301 duties that took effect in July 2026, roughly 45% of India’s US-bound exports remain outside the tariff base because of product exemptions, and the remaining 55% face the new 10% duty on top of standard MFN rates.

Share of India’s US-bound exports, July 2026 regime
Commerce Ministry statement, 25 July 2026.

GTRI estimates about 70% of India’s US-bound exports would face regular duties plus the new levy once broader sectoral effects are counted, including garments, machinery, chemicals, plastics, leather goods, gems and furniture.

Competitiveness

India lost its tariff advantage, then partly recovered it

CareEdge’s assessment is blunt: India’s relative tariff advantage over peers such as Vietnam, Indonesia and South Korea effectively reversed once the 25% duty landed, with the Russian oil penalty adding to it. The February 2026 reset to 18% restored India to roughly parity with, or slightly below, most competitors.

Current US reciprocal tariff rate, India versus competitors
Economy-wide rates only. Excludes MFN duties, Section 232 and Section 301 measures.

At 18% India sits below Bangladesh and Cambodia at 19%, the EU at 15% and China at 20% on the announced February 2026 reset. The current operating rate of 10% under the Section 301 regime applies to many partners alike, so relative differentiation is temporarily narrower.

The textile exclusion that did lasting damage

The most durable competitive loss was in textiles. The Section 301 process included a special mechanism allowing a specified volume of textile and apparel imports from selected economies using US-origin cotton and fibre to enter at lower rates. India was excluded, while Bangladesh, Cambodia, Indonesia and Malaysia qualified. CITI warned that the differential treatment “risks diverting sourcing orders for textile and apparel items away from India”, against a US market worth about $11 billion a year for Indian suppliers, and against roughly $8 billion of Bangladeshi apparel exports and $4 billion each from Indonesia and Cambodia.

The impact depends on how much qualifying US-origin fabric those rivals use, which is not yet known. But the asymmetry is structural: India pays the full 10% while competitors may pay less.

Trade & the external balance

Exports recovered; the deficit widened anyway

Merchandise exports grew strongly in the year after the peak. But imports grew faster, so the merchandise trade deficit widened materially over the same period.

Reading the recovery honestly

Exports up 17.04% for April–July 2026-27 is a strong number, but it is not evidence that the tariffs were costless. The merchandise trade deficit over the same period was $118.60 billion, against $96.66 billion a year earlier — a 22.7% widening. The tariff story is one sector of a much larger trade position that is being driven by import growth, energy costs and gold flows far more than by Washington.

Macro context

The oil exposure that started it all

The punitive duty was triggered by Russian crude purchases, and that trade continued through the entire tariff episode. India bought $40.8 billion of Russian crude in FY26, accounting for 30.3% of its total crude imports. By July 2026 imports were at a record high for a second consecutive month, and GTRI reported that 53% of that volume moved on “shadow sanctions”.

What changed was the economics, not the volume. The Ural-Brent price differential narrowed to about $3 per barrel from an average of roughly $20 in 2023, which is why CareEdge judges that diversifying away from Russian oil would have had only a minimal effect on the current account.

This is the bind at the centre of the episode. The duty was imposed over a purchase that had become far less economically significant, at a time when switching suppliers would have raised the import bill for very little gain.

Second-order

Consequences beyond the customs line

Effects that are harder to measure but were widely reported.

💼

Margin compression, not just lost volume

Where exporters held on to volume, they did so by discounting. Reuters reported that after the August 2025 hike, sectors including textiles, jewellery and shrimp were hit although discounts helped exporters retain some share of the market. The cost shows up in margin rather than in shipment count.

👥👥👥

Employment exposure in labour-intensive sectors

India Today’s reporting on the 50% peak documented the fear of large revenue losses, layoffs and closure in export-oriented units, with apparel and textiles the most labour-intensive lines at risk. Sector-level employment data disaggregated by tariff exposure is not routinely published, so this remains the least quantified consequence.

📈

Order-book and investment uncertainty

Beyond realised losses sits unrealised ones. A tariff regime that can move from 10% to 50% and back within a year, and that has been litigated in the Supreme Court, is difficult to plan against. Reuters framed the holdout position as carrying its own cost: prolonged uncertainty for businesses.

🏦

Buyer substitution in both directions

Some Indian buyers switched to other suppliers, and some US buyers switched away from India. Frozen shrimp still recorded growth despite tariffs, because US demand for Indian Litopenaeus vannamei and Black Tiger varieties held up. Where a product is genuinely differentiated, tariffs are absorbed.

⚖

Diversification had measurable effects

Textile exports grew in more than 120 destinations between April 2025 and February 2026, with strong gains in the UAE, UK, Germany, Spain, Japan, Egypt, Nigeria, Senegal and Sudan. Gems and jewellery saw strong growth in the UAE, Hong Kong, Australia and Canada. Diversification is the clearest structural legacy of the episode.

⚠️

The overhang has not lifted

The Lindsey Graham Act, passed on 18 September 2026, authorises tariffs of up to 100% on exports from India. Nothing has been imposed under it. But it is the reason India wants a locked-in deal, and the reason it has been willing to wait.
