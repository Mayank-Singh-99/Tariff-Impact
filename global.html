The wider board

India is one board in a much larger game

The US tariff regime is not an India story with other countries attached. It is one arena in a simultaneous contest over rare earths, over who supplies apparel, over whether allies can hold a deal with Washington, and over who gets accused of helping Chinese firms route around the whole structure. India's position in each of those contests matters more to its exporters than the Indian duty rate does.

The other players

Four responses, four different theories of power

China retaliated upstream, where it holds an irreplaceable position. The EU responded legally and procedurally, because it had signed a deal and expected it to be honoured. Vietnam, Cambodia, Indonesia and Malaysia captured orders and then received preferential quota access that India was denied. Japan is simply exposed, caught in a diplomatic dispute it did not choose.

The carve-out that matters most

India was excluded from the textile quota

The July 2026 Section 301 measure imposed duties of 10% and 12.5% on goods from 60 trading partners. Alongside it came tariff-rate quotas letting specified textile and apparel shipments from Bangladesh, Cambodia, Indonesia and Malaysia enter the US free of that duty, provided they used US-origin cotton and fibre. India was not included.

This is the single most consequential detail in the sectoral story, because it reverses the usual logic. India is not being penalised relative to nobody. It is being penalised relative to the four economies that compete with it directly for the same apparel orders, while simultaneously absorbing the same 10% duty.

Bangladesh

Exported about $8B of apparel to the US in 2024. Stayed the second-largest US supplier and ahead of China for a fourth consecutive month in early 2026, though its own US exports also declined.

Indonesia & Cambodia

Each shipped roughly $4B of apparel to the US. In early 2026 Indonesia grew 2.3% and Cambodia 14.1%, on cost, lead times and buyer confidence.

Vietnam

Retained the top supplier position with $5.16bn, the largest single gainer from the China-plus-one shift.

What CITI said, and why it is the right complaint

The Confederation of Indian Textile Industry put it plainly: the differential treatment risks diverting sourcing orders away from India. India exports roughly $11B of textile and apparel to the US annually — more than Bangladesh and more than Indonesia and Cambodia combined. Losing share in a market where the competitor set has just been handed a duty exemption is a much larger risk than the headline 10% rate. The exemption is conditional on the share of garments made with US-origin cotton and fabric, which is the part that is hardest to fix quickly, since Indian spinning capacity is built for Indian cotton.

For scale

  • India textile and apparel exports to the US: about $11bn a year
  • The Section 301 duty India now faces on the same products: 10%
  • Bangladesh, Indonesia, Cambodia and Malaysia: potentially zero on qualifying shipments
  • GTRI estimate: about 70% of Indian exports to the US face regular duties plus the new levy

The GTRI figure is a different measurement from the 55% used elsewhere on this site. It covers all Indian exports to the US, not just the tariffed subset, and it comes from a third-party estimate rather than a Commerce Ministry breakdown.

The leverage asymmetry

Why rare earths matter more to India than tariffs do

The United States can raise a duty on Indian goods. It cannot replace Chinese rare earth processing at any price, on any timescale, because the separation and magnet manufacturing capacity simply does not exist elsewhere. That asymmetry is the reason Beijing has leverage in the tariff fight that Washington does not, and it is the reason a November 2025 deal involving soybean purchases and rare earth access was struck at all.

●

The controls

China restricts exports of dual-use materials including rare earths, gallium, germanium, graphite and magnets. Controls were extended to Japan explicitly tied to Tokyo’s position on Taiwan, and in June 2026 ten US entities were added to the export control list, including MP Materials and USA Rare Earth.

●

The squeeze

Chinese customs data showed no gallium, dysprosium, terbium or yttrium sent to Japan in June 2026. Rare earth exports to Japan fell 51% year on year in the first half of 2026. Overall Chinese rare earth exports fell 16%, so the targeting is deliberate rather than incidental.

✓

The truce

A November 2025 agreement saw Beijing commit to eliminating rare earth export controls and to purchase US soybeans and sorghum. The truce was due to expire in November 2026, and both sides were negotiating an extension as of September 2026.

What this means for India specifically

India imports a large share of its electronics components and, since the rare earth controls tightened, has faced supply bottlenecks reported as directly affecting Indian manufacturing. A country that is a net importer of the very inputs Washington is trying to secure has limited ability to play the two sides against each other. The G7 critical minerals discussions and the India–EFTA and India–EU agreements all run into this constraint: diversification of export markets does nothing about diversification of critical input supply. For India, the export tariff story and the input security story are pulling in opposite directions.

Allied friction

The EU signed a deal and then was told it had not complied

In 2025 Washington and Brussels reached an agreement at Turnberry. In May 2026 the US raised tariffs on EU-made cars from 15% to 25%, contending that Brussels had not been complying with what was agreed. The EU response was to invoke the deal rather than retaliate, which tells you something about how much room allies actually have.

Trade defence runs both ways

It is worth remembering that European trade policy is not uniformly open. The EU imposed countervailing duties of 3.7% to 8.1% on Indian optical fibre cables in June 2025, and the India–EU FTA concluded in January 2026 reserves significant sensitive categories with phased elimination, tariff reductions and TRQs rather than immediate zero duty.

For an Indian exporter, the difference between a US tariff and an EU safeguard is the remedy: a US duty is paid to the US Treasury, while an EU countervailing duty is collected by the EU and is theoretically recoverable by the importer. The former is a permanent margin cost; the latter is a price preference that can be traded away.

The G7 minerals problem

G7 trade ministers met in Paris in May 2026 to coordinate on critical mineral supply, with France treating it as a presidency deliverable. The reported consensus was agreement on reducing reliance on China and disagreement on how to achieve it.

This is the fault line running through the India relationship too. India is a beneficiary of the China-plus-one sourcing shift, an importer of the critical inputs that shift depends on, and a signatory to an Initiative on Critical and Emerging Technology with Washington. Those three positions are compatible in principle and awkward in practice.

The India-specific risk

Being named in a transshipment case

Of everything on this page, this carries the most direct tariff consequence for India, because it is a live Section 301 matter rather than a historical episode. The allegation is that Chinese exporters are routing goods through more than forty countries, and that the Pune–Gujarat–Chennai engineering corridor is a specific channel for pumps and compressors.

The scale of the allegation

The White House has placed India among the countries at highest risk of being used to circumvent US tariffs on Chinese goods, citing an estimated $67bn of goods allegedly transshipped through India, Mexico and Vietnam in 2025. That figure is an estimate, not a verified quantity.

India’s response

New Delhi has requested further information from the US on the claims and has itself asked the Global Trade Research Initiative to examine Indian manufacturing and export data to test the allegation. The GTRI has advised India to do exactly that.

Why it is awkward

India exports the pumps, compressors and engineering goods that the China-plus-one shift is built on. Being the supplier of the machinery that makes the shift work, while also being the destination market for the goods it moves, is structurally ambiguous. That ambiguity is the vulnerability.

The structural problem

Western markets cannot be replaced quickly, emerging markets cannot yet fill the Chinese order book, and trading partners will quietly avoid blacklisted Chinese firms rather than risk falling foul of US rules. Those conditions are what make evasion attractive, and they are also what make the accusation credible. India’s position is that the enforcement logic is unfair, because a genuine Indian exporter and a transshipment channel look identical in customs data until somebody investigates the underlying firms. That is a real argument, and it has not yet been tested by a published determination.
